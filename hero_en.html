Li Yougan was the younger brother of Li Youxing, the noted painter, art educator, and founder of the Sichuan Fine Arts Institute — eight years his senior. Youngest of three brothers, he was also the boldest: as a teenager he ran off alone to Southeast Asia, and later secretly travelled to Jianqiao, Hangzhou, to sit the entrance exam for the Central Aviation School, becoming one of China's first fighter pilots.

When the Battle of Shanghai broke out, his unit flew sortie after sortie until men and machines alike were spent. On September 18, 1937 — the sixth anniversary of the Mukden Incident — he took part in a Mid-Autumn night raid on Japanese forces in Shanghai. Flying a Hawk III biplane already obsolete against Japanese defenses, he attacked an enemy flagship anchored on the Huangpu River and was struck down by concentrated anti-aircraft fire.

He left no children. He was twenty-four. His brother Li Youxing travelled to Nanjing to gather his belongings, and among them found nothing — not even a photograph — to suggest he had ever been in love.

Life Story

Twenty-Four Years, One Complete Life

1913
Born at No. 38 Wuding Hou Xikouwai, Beiping
His father Li Baimin worked in Beijing, and all three brothers grew up there. Eight years younger than his second brother Li Youxing, he was raised largely under his brother's care, taken on walks through Fragrant Hills and Jade Spring Hill.
c. 1928
Enrolled at the Fragrant Hills Charity School
His brother arranged for him to board there. Every Sunday, when Li Youxing cycled out to visit, he would run out to meet him and show him around.
c. 1929
Ran away to Southeast Asia for three years
While their mother was out, he took the family's savings and left for Singapore with a schoolmate, without a word. He vanished for three years and returned penniless. His brother later wrote: the ordeal left him "bolder, and with broader eyes."
c. 1933
Secretly sat the entrance exam at Jianqiao, Hangzhou
The Central Aviation School at Jianqiao was then China's only formal aviation academy. He applied without telling the family, and entered as a cadet of the 5th Class, Section B, beginning his career as a pilot.
1936
Crashed in training; a last photograph in Shanghai
He crashed during a training flight and lost several front teeth. While being treated in Shanghai, he sat for a family photograph with his brother's family — one of the few images of him that survive.
Aug 1937
The Battle of Shanghai begins; continuous combat
The battle broke out on August 13. His squadron flew repeated sorties against Japanese ships, docks, and positions. With pilots in desperately short supply, he flew in nearly every mission.
Sept 18
1937
The Mid-Autumn night raid — fallen in the sky
On the night of September 18, he flew with Li Guidan, Liu Zhesheng, Wang Yuanbo, Gong Yeti, and Ba Qingzheng in the first wave, striking Japanese forces at Huishan Wharf and warships on the Huangpu River. Struck by anti-aircraft fire, he fell at the age of twenty-four.
Combat Record

The Night of September 18, 1937 — Mid-Autumn Offensive

From the Taiwan Martyrs' Shrine archive · Air Force combat record, Battle of Shanghai
The Mid-Autumn Night Raid, September 18

On September 17, 1937, Madame Chiang Kai-shek told the officers of the Aeronautical Commission: "Gentlemen, you need not be told what tomorrow is. It is the sixth anniversary of the Mukden Incident. To wash away that shame, the Generalissimo orders the Air Force to strike the enemy its hardest blow…"

September 18 fell, that year, on the Mid-Autumn Festival. Pilots stationed in Nanjing who had fasted every September 18 for six years in memory of national humiliation now received their orders — and resolved to fight through the night, wave after wave, toward Shanghai.

At 19:30 that evening, the first wave launched: Squadron leader Li Guidan, with Liu Zhesheng, Wang Yuanbo, Gong Yeti, Li Yougan, and Ba Qingzheng, bombing a Japanese munitions depot and warships anchored off Shanghai.

Over the course of the night, Chinese aircraft flew 21 sorties, bombing Japanese forces in Shanghai continuously until dawn — destroying over 7 million yuan worth of munitions, and striking the Japanese cruiser Izumo with three 500-pound bombs.

Li Yougan's own mission was the most perilous of the plan: flying a Hawk III biplane, a design already a generation obsolete, against a heavily defended enemy flagship. He was struck by anti-aircraft fire and fell over the Huangpu River.

His name appears, marked in red for the fallen, on the roll of the Central Aviation School's 5th Class: "September 1937 — killed by anti-aircraft fire while bombing Japanese positions at Huishan Wharf."

Every flight-track cadet of that class died in service. China's first generation of fighter pilots very nearly ceased to exist before 1942 — flying aircraft far outmatched by the enemy's, they traded their lives, on average at twenty-three, simply to buy their country time.

A Brother's Grief

From "In Memory of Yougan"

Three months after Li Yougan's death, his brother Li Youxing — then travelling south to relocate his art school inland — stopped in Changde, Hunan, and wrote a long memoir, In Memory of Yougan (《忆幹弟》). It remains the only sustained written record of Li Yougan's life, and one of the family's most treasured possessions.

Li Youxing · December 22, 1937 · Wuling Garden, Changde, Hunan

Chapter One — Brothers (excerpt)

I loved my brother's courage, his warmth, his high spirits — and I loved even his recklessness. Oh, my dearest Yougan, when shall we meet again?

… I took him to Fragrant Hills. I covered him with my own coat as he fell asleep, contented, and found in his peaceful face a great comfort. He never once blamed me for the hardship, only thanked me for the outing. My brother was truly brave.

Chapter Three — Eternal Skies, Blue Blood (excerpt)

… the messenger reached the door and announced, in a loud voice, that this man had died in battle over Shanghai on the night of the eighteenth.

I went cold all over, and fell silent, not knowing whether to believe it. … In a low voice I admitted to myself: the news was almost certainly true. A man's name is not so easily mistaken.

… Soldiers who had known him, seeing the likeness in my face, watched me with quiet sorrow. They all said he had been good-natured, open-hearted — what a pity, they said, what a pity.

… My eyes too full of tears to see clearly, I sorted through his scattered belongings. I found his spare dental plate. I gathered a small packet of loose photographs to keep…

Oh, my dear brother — I loved you so, and this is how you were lost to me. When victory comes, I will go to the banks of the Huangpu, to Yangshupu Wharf, and pour libations for your loyal soul.

— Li Youxing, elder brother, written in the winter of 1937

This diary survived the Cultural Revolution only by chance — confiscated along with several others during a house raid, it resurfaced, almost miraculously, among a handful of "returned items" more than a decade later.

Li Youxing had once made his brother a photo album by hand, filled with images of him in uniform. That album did not survive. Of Li Yougan's photographs, only three remain in the family's keeping, and none show him in uniform — a single such photograph survives only in official archives.

Memorial Sites

Remembered on Both Shores

Li Yougan is permanently commemorated at two official memorials. His great-nephew, who led the family on a pilgrimage to Nanjing, later wrote: "On both shores of the strait, there are people who remember you and your comrades."

Nanjing Anti-Japanese Aviation Martyrs' Memorial
Northern slope of Purple Mountain, Nanjing
Founded in 1932 and rebuilt several times after war and upheaval, the memorial inscribes the names of 4,296 Chinese and allied aviation martyrs. Li Yougan has a memorial tablet here, and his uniformed portrait is on display. The family has visited to pay respects.
Tablet inscription
A native of Jiangyou, Sichuan; graduate of the Central Aviation School, 5th Class Section B; pilot of the 22nd Squadron, 4th Group; killed by anti-aircraft fire on the night of September 18, 1937, raiding Japanese forces at Huishan Wharf, Shanghai. Posthumously promoted to Lieutenant.
Taiwan National Revolutionary Martyrs' Shrine
Yuanshan Park, Taipei
The shrine honors nearly 400,000 fallen souls, each recorded by name, birth date, ancestral home, and place of death. Li Yougan's uniformed photograph and service records are held here — it was this very photograph that let the family rediscover his image as a soldier.
Archival record
His registered address reads "No. 38 Wuding Hou Xikouwai, Beiping" — the family home of his grandfather Li Baimin, where Li Yougan was born.
Family Background

Three Brothers of the Li Family, Shuangban, Zitong

The Li family of Shuangban, Zitong, Sichuan, had by the second generation moved to Beiping — and, by Li Youxing's own account, all three brothers were "rebels at heart." The eldest, Li Youdu, was a May Fourth-era student at Peking University's English department who marched in the streets rather than study. The second, Li Youxing, refused to go into business as his father wished, insisted on painting instead, and travelled all the way to the Lyon art school in France — arriving too late to see his father one last time. The youngest, Li Yougan, outdid them both: stealing away to Southeast Asia, secretly enrolling at the aviation academy — each defining act of his life was done first and explained never. Fortunately, their father closed his eyes before living to see his youngest son's escapades.

Their father, Li Baimin, died young and never saw his youngest son become a fighter pilot. Li Youxing raised him, in effect, as a father would — walking him through snow at Fragrant Hills, naming crops for him at Jade Spring Hill, carrying him home through the dark outside Xizhi Gate. So when the news of his death came, it was Li Youxing who grieved most in all the world.

On the album he made by hand for his brother, Li Youxing later wrote, in his own hand: "In memory of my lost brother, Qunyi" — Qunyi being Li Yougan's courtesy name. That album was destroyed in the raids of 1966. The uniformed photographs it held have never been seen again.
